Start with your home's age

Olympia Fields is a small village of about 1,900 housing units, and history here runs deep. Permanent homes were built west of the railroad tracks beginning around 1919, though your own house may be much newer. The practical step is the same, so check the year on your deed or tax record before you plan any pipe work.

The build year tells us what materials to expect. Houses from before the 1960s may have galvanized steel supply pipes that rust from the inside and lose flow. Later houses may have copper, and newer ones plastic, each with its own failure points.

Leaks you cannot see

Mature trees and older yards make the supply line to the house hard to follow. A water meter that keeps turning with every fixture off means the water is going somewhere. Leak detection uses acoustic listening, thermal imaging and pressure tests to narrow the location before anyone opens a wall.

Slab leaks, wall leaks and buried yard leaks all behave differently. We cannot promise to find a leak without access, but the narrower the search, the smaller the hole.

Before We Arrive in Olympia Fields

Locate the shutoff

In an older Olympia Fields house the main valve may sit behind a basement panel or beside a meter pit, and it can be stiff from years of rest. Turn it slowly clockwise. If it will not move, do not force it.

Switch off nearby circuits

Wet plaster and old wiring are a poor mix. Flip the breakers serving the soaked room from a dry spot, and keep a flashlight handy so you are not guessing in the dark.

Protect what you can

Lift rugs, books and keepsakes away from the drip line, and set a bucket under any ceiling bulge. A small hole in a bulging ceiling can release trapped water in a controlled stream.
