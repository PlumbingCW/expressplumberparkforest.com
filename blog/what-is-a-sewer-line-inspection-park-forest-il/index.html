What Is a Sewer Line Inspection? A Park Forest, IL Homeowner Guide

A sewer line inspection sends a waterproof camera through your main drain so a technician can see cracks, roots, sags and blockages without digging.

Camera on a push cableLocator mappingWritten reportHome-buying use
By the Park Forest Express Plumbing team · Published October 30, 2026

A sewer line inspection is a camera survey of the pipe that carries waste from your house to the street. A technician feeds a small waterproof camera on a flexible cable into the line, usually through a cleanout, and watches live video of the pipe walls. The goal is to find damage, roots, bellies and blockages before they cause a backup.

Most inspections take a modest part of a visit and leave your yard intact. You get a clear answer about what is wrong and where, so repairs can be limited to the failed section. It is a look inside the pipe, and not a repair.

How the visit usually runs

Find a way into the line

The camera enters through a cleanout, a capped fitting on the main drain. If none exists, the technician may pull a toilet or use a roof vent, and a missing cleanout is itself a finding.

Run the camera downstream

A push cable carries the camera toward the street while a light shows the pipe interior. The technician narrates what appears on the monitor and pauses at anything unusual.

Locate the problem on the surface

The camera head carries a transmitter. A handheld locator above ground picks up its signal and reports the depth, so a crew knows where to dig if a repair is needed.

Record and review

Video and notes are saved, and the technician explains the findings in plain language. Ask for a copy of the video, since it helps with quotes and with sellers.

Reading the findings

Pipe material changes what the camera sees. Clay tile shows offset joints and root hairs, cast iron shows flaking and scale, and plastic pipe shows sags or deformed walls. The table pairs common findings with what they usually mean.

Common camera findings and typical next steps
What the camera showsWhat it usually meansTypical next step
Roots entering at jointsJoints have opened enough to let moisture and roots inCut the roots, then watch for regrowth or repair the joint
Standing water in a low sectionA belly where the pipe has settledMonitor, or replace that section if waste collects
Cracked or broken pipeSoil or ground movement has fractured the wallSpot repair or replacement
Heavy scale or flakingAging cast iron narrowing the boreCleaning, and a plan for eventual replacement
Offset or separated jointTwo pipe sections no longer line upSpot repair
Clean walls, steady flowNo visible defect at the timeKeep a copy as a baseline

A belly is not always urgent. Water can sit in a shallow sag for years and the line still drains. The record matters more than a single frame, since a repeat camera run in a year or two shows whether the defect is growing.

Why buyers order one before closing

A home inspector sees fixtures and visible piping, not the buried line. If a house is older or has large trees near the line, a sewer camera run before closing can reveal costly surprises while you still have room to negotiate. A replaced main can be a major expense, so many buyers want the facts first.

Sellers sometimes order their own inspection to head off questions. Either way, ask who owns the report, and confirm the video covers the full run from the house to the connection at the street. A clip that stops partway answers only part of the question.

What a camera cannot tell you

The camera sees only the pipe it can reach. It cannot see through a full blockage, so a line packed with debris may need cleaning first with a hydro jet or cable machine before the inspection can continue. Sharp bends can stop the camera, and a section beyond a collapse stays hidden.

  • It cannot judge soil conditions around the pipe.
  • It cannot measure slope to an exact number the way a survey instrument can, though it shows low spots.
  • It cannot see inside the municipal main past the connection.
  • It cannot predict how long an old pipe will last, only its condition today.
  • It may miss a hairline crack that closes when the pipe is dry.

Take the report as a snapshot. If a finding is unclear, ask the technician to show the footage again or run a second pass after cleaning.

Before the technician arrives

Find your cleanout and clear a path to it. Skip heavy flushing right before the visit, because a wet, full line can hide defects, and tell the technician about past clogs, odors or slow drains so they know where to look.

When a problem is found, the next step is a fix sized to the damage. Our sewer inspection service covers the camera work, and when a section has failed, sewer line repair options range from spot repair to replacement depending on what the video shows.

Park Forest note: sewer backups and permits

The Village of Park Forest says urban flooding happens when rain falls faster than the ground can absorb it and sewers cannot handle the runoff, which can cause sewage backups in basements. The Village is replacing its wastewater lines, which are the public mains in the street. A camera inspection covers your private line up to the connection, so it tells you whether a backup is a problem on your side or one to report to the Village.

The Village flooding page also asks residents not to flush or pour fats, oils, wipes or paper towels, following Metropolitan Water Reclamation District guidance. Those items catch on rough pipe and roots, which is exactly where a camera often finds trouble. Sewer repair permits at the Building Department, 350 Victory Drive, cover installing cleanouts, and a contractor must be Park Forest licensed. Nearby, the same questions apply in Olympia Fields, where permanent homes date to around 1919, and in University Park, incorporated in 1967, so check each home's build year to judge how old the pipe may be.

Questions we hear

Many inspections are done in a modest part of a visit once the technician reaches the line. Time depends on the length of the run, how easy the cleanout is to reach, and whether a clog must be cleared first. A long or blocked line takes longer.

No digging is needed for the camera run. The camera goes in through a cleanout or fixture. Digging comes only later, if a repair is needed, and the locator helps limit the dig to the failed section.

It can show cracks, open joints and root intrusion, which are common leak points. It cannot always confirm that water is leaving the pipe. For water supply leaks inside walls or slabs, other methods are used.

It is a smart step for older homes or lots with mature trees, since the buried line is outside a standard home inspection. Ask for the full video and make sure it covers the line out to the street connection.

Sewer repair permits at the Village Building Department cover installing cleanouts, so ask them before work starts. Contractors in the village must be Park Forest licensed. Permits are dropped off or mailed, not taken by email or online. A cleanout also makes future camera work easier.
