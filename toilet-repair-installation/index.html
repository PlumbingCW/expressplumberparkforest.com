Why a toilet keeps running, and what usually fixes it

A running toilet wastes water all day and often rings up the water bill before anyone notices. The cause is almost always inside the tank, where a few cheap parts wear out after years of use.

Worn flapper

The rubber flapper seals the tank outlet. When it warps or gets slimy, water seeps into the bowl and the tank keeps topping off.

Tired fill valve

If the fill valve will not shut off, water rises to the overflow tube and drains away. Replacing the valve is a routine repair.

Bad chain or float

A chain that is too short holds the flapper open, and a misadjusted float lets the tank overfill. Both are quick adjustments.

Cracked tank or bowl

A hairline crack in porcelain does not repair well. Replacement is the honest answer once the tank or bowl is leaking from the body.

Leaks at the base and a toilet that rocks

Every toilet sits on a wax ring, a thick gasket that seals the bowl outlet to the drain flange. When that seal fails, water seeps out at the floor with each flush. You may see staining, soft flooring, a musty smell, or a damp spot on the ceiling below.

Closet bolts hold the toilet to the flange, and they should be snug but not cranked down, because overtightening can crack the porcelain. A rocking toilet breaks the wax seal over time. We reset it with a new ring, check that the flange sits level on top of the finished floor, and replace corroded bolts.

Replacing a toilet in an older bathroom

A toilet with a cracked bowl, a chronic clog from an old design, or repeated seal failures is usually better replaced. The big measurements are the rough-in, which is the distance from the finished wall to the center of the drain, and the flange height.

Twelve inches is the most common rough-in, but ten and fourteen inch layouts exist, so we measure before you buy a new toilet. A flange should sit on top of the finished floor, not below it, or the seal will not hold. Taller comfort height bowls sit a few inches above standard ones and are easier on knees. Water-saving models use less water per flush than older tanks, which adds up on a family's water bill over the years.

Is it a toilet leak or something else?

A silent leak is the sneakiest kind. Drop a few drops of food coloring into the tank and wait about twenty minutes without flushing. If color shows in the bowl, the flapper is leaking.

Water that appears around the toilet may also come from the supply line, the shutoff valve or a pipe in the wall. Winters in the south suburbs bring freeze and thaw cycles, and a supply line in an exterior wall can crack or loosen. If we cannot find the source at the fixture, leak detection is the next step, and for a burst line the answer is burst pipe repair.

Permits and licensing in Park Forest

The Village of Park Forest says all contractors working in the village must be Park Forest licensed, and it adopts the Illinois State Plumbing Code. A simple toilet swap is usually minor, but moving a drain or adding fixtures can need a permit. Call the Building Department at Village Hall to confirm.

What affects the cost of toilet repair or replacement

Repair or replace

Swapping a flapper or fill valve is a small job. Resetting a toilet with a new wax ring takes more labor, and a full replacement adds the fixture itself.

The toilet you choose

Standard two-piece bowls, comfort height models and one-piece designs vary widely in price, and you can supply your own if it fits the rough-in.

Flange and floor condition

A damaged flange or soft subfloor adds repair work that we can only judge after the toilet is lifted.

Access and bathroom layout

Tight spaces, old shutoff valves that must be replaced and difficult supply lines all change the time on site.

Timing of the visit

Calls that need a visit at night or on a weekend are priced differently from a scheduled daytime appointment.
