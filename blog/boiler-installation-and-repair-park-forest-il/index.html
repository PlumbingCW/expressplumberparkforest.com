Boiler Installation and Repair in Park Forest, IL: Fix It or Replace It?

Repair makes sense for a failed part on a boiler in decent shape. Replacement makes sense when the vessel leaks, repairs repeat, or the system no longer fits the house.

Call (855) 575-2890

By the Park Forest Express Plumbing team · Published October 30, 2026

A boiler heats water, or makes steam, and sends it through pipes to radiators, baseboards or in-floor loops. Repair is the better choice when one part has failed, such as a circulator pump, a thermostat, a zone valve or an igniter. Replacement is the better choice when the heat exchanger or the vessel itself leaks, since that failure cannot be patched in a lasting way.

Most decisions sit between those two ends. The table below sorts the common situations, and the sections after it explain what a new installation involves so you know what to ask.

A quick way to sort the decision

Age matters, but it is not a verdict. Many cast iron boilers run for decades when maintained, while some lighter units wear out sooner. A well-kept older boiler with a simple fault can be worth repairing, and a young unit that leaks may not be.

Signs a boiler needs attention now

No heat or uneven heat

One room stays cold or the radiators are warm at the bottom and cool at the top. Air in the loop or a failed zone valve are common causes.

Banging or kettle-like noises

Mineral scale or low water can cause rumbling, and trapped air causes knocking. Ignoring it can damage the heat exchanger.

Pressure that swings

Pressure that climbs fast or drops often can mean a failed expansion tank or a slow leak in the loop.

Water around the base

Dripping from a fitting may be a simple repair. Water from the vessel itself generally means the boiler is finished.

Safety first

Boilers burn fuel and can produce carbon monoxide if venting fails. Keep working CO alarms near sleeping areas. If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 before anything else, and do not relight a pilot yourself.

What a proper installation includes

A good installation starts with sizing. The installer estimates the home's heat loss from its size, insulation and windows, then picks a boiler to match. Going by the old boiler's size alone is a common mistake, because houses change after weatherstripping, window replacements and added insulation.

Measure the heat load

A room-by-room heat loss estimate shows how much output the house needs on a cold design day. An oversized boiler short-cycles and wears faster.

Check radiation and piping

The radiators, baseboards and pipe sizes must be able to deliver the heat. Old piping may need repair, balancing or air removal.

Plan the venting

High-efficiency condensing boilers vent in plastic pipe and need a condensate drain. Older atmospheric units use a chimney, and a liner may be needed so exhaust does not condense in the flue.

Install controls and safeties

This includes the expansion tank, a pressure relief valve, a low water cutoff where required, air separation and the zone controls.

Fill, purge and test

The loop is filled, air is purged, and the system is tested for leaks and combustion. The installer then walks through the controls and the final inspection.

The expansion tank deserves a note. Heated water expands, and a closed loop needs somewhere for that volume to go. A waterlogged tank is a common cause of pressure swings and relief valve drips.

Zones, controls and the rest of the plumbing

Zoning splits the house into areas with their own thermostats, using zone valves or separate circulators. It lets you heat a bedroom wing less than the living area, and it can reduce wasted heat. A new installation is the right time to fix a layout that never worked well.

Boiler work also touches plumbing, including makeup water lines, backflow preventers, drains and gas piping. Our plumbing installation page explains the wider scope, and it is wise to ask who handles each part when you compare proposals.

Questions to ask before you approve a proposal

  • How was the boiler size chosen, and was a heat loss estimate done?
  • What will the venting look like, and where will condensate drain?
  • Is the expansion tank new, and is a low water cutoff included?
  • Who pulls the permit and arranges the inspection?
  • What happens to the old boiler, and is there any asbestos on old pipe wrap that must be handled by a specialist?

The asbestos question matters on very old systems, where insulation on pipes or the boiler can contain it. A technician should flag suspect material and not disturb it. Call (855) 575-2890 if you want a tech to look at your system before deciding.

Park Forest note: permits and licensing

The Village of Park Forest Building Department, at 350 Victory Drive, requires that all contractors doing work in the village be Park Forest licensed. A permit is needed if you do the work yourself or hire a contractor, and a homeowner doing their own work submits a waiver form. Questions we hear

Look for water that appears under or at the base of the boiler body, with rust streaks or staining around seams. A dripping pipe fitting or relief valve is a different, more fixable problem. A technician can tell which one you have.

Often the venting changes. Condensing units vent in plastic pipe through a wall and need a condensate drain. A boiler staying on a chimney may need a liner. The installer should check this on site.

Common causes are a waterlogged or failed expansion tank, a slow leak, or an auto-fill valve that is not seating. Pressure that changes by a lot is worth having checked before it opens the relief valve.
