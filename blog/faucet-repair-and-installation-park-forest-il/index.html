Faucet Repair vs Replacement and Installation in Park Forest

Most dripping faucets need a new cartridge, washer or seat rather than a new fixture, but a corroded body or a worn finish is a good reason to replace it.

By the Park Forest Express Plumbing team · Published October 30, 2026

Repair it or replace it

Repair the faucet when it drips or squeaks but the body is solid, the finish is intact and parts are still sold for it. Replace it when the body is pitted or cracked, the spout leaks at the base, or the faucet is old enough that no cartridge fits. A new cartridge or washer is a short job, while a full faucet swap takes most of an afternoon for a first-timer.

The deciding factor is usually the part that leaks. A drip from the spout tip is typically a worn seal inside, and a puddle at the base points to worn O-rings or a cracked body. Corrosion that you can scrape with a fingernail means the metal itself is failing.

Know the cartridge type before you shop

Four designs cover most home faucets. Knowing which you own saves a second trip to the hardware store, and the brand name is usually printed under the handle or on the box you may still have.

Compression

Two handles that you tighten to stop the water. A rubber washer wears out against the seat, which is why these drip. Washers are cheap and easy to swap.

Ball

A single handle over a slotted ball, common in older kitchens. Leaks usually come from worn seats and springs, and kits replace them together.

Cartridge

A single or double handle moving a brass or plastic cartridge. The cartridge is the whole mechanism, so replacing it fixes most drips.

Ceramic disc

Two ceramic plates slide to meter flow, found in many modern faucets. They last long, and when they leak the cylinder is replaced as one piece.

Tools and parts to gather first

  • Adjustable wrench and a set of hex keys for set screws under the handle.
  • A basin wrench, which reaches the cramped nuts behind a sink bowl. A regular wrench cannot get up there.
  • Plumber's tape, a bucket, a flashlight and a rag to cover the drain so small parts do not fall in.
  • New braided stainless supply lines if the old ones are stiff, kinked or more than a few years old.
  • Plumber's putty or the gasket that comes with the new faucet, depending on the base design.

Installing a new faucet, start to finish

Shut off the water

Turn the two angle stop valves under the sink clockwise. Open the old faucet to drain the lines and confirm the water is off. If a stop will not turn or leaks when moved, stop there, because forcing an old valve can start a worse leak.

Disconnect and remove the old faucet

Loosen the supply nuts and the mounting nuts with a basin wrench. Lift the faucet out, then scrape the old putty or gasket from the sink surface so the new base sits flat.

Mount the new faucet

Feed the new faucet through the holes and tighten the mounting nuts from underneath. Check that the spout is straight and that the base is snug before you finish tightening.

Connect the supply lines

Hand-tighten the braided lines to the faucet and to the stop valves, then add a quarter turn with a wrench. Over-tightening crushes the rubber washer inside the nut and causes the very leak you are trying to avoid.

Flush and test

Remove the aerator, open the faucet and both stops, and let the water run for a minute to push out debris. Then replace the aerator and check every joint with a dry paper towel.

Practical tip

Take a photo of the plumbing under the sink before you start, and bring the old supply line and cartridge to the store. A matching part in your hand beats a guess about sizes, and the photo helps if you forget how a mounting plate fits back together.

Signs the job is bigger than the faucet

Call a plumber if the shutoff valves will not close fully, if the sink sits on a rotted cabinet floor, or if the supply stubs coming out of the wall are corroded or loose. Those parts are behind the faucet, and replacing a faucet on top of a failing valve just moves the leak. We cannot judge the pipe behind a wall without opening it, but we can tell you whether the visible parts are sound.

Our faucet repair service covers worn cartridges, seats, and fixtures that leak under the sink. For a new faucet, a relocated sink, or work that touches supply and drain pipes, plumbing installation is the better match, since those jobs involve fitting and sometimes a permit.

Park Forest note: licenses and permits when you hire

The Building Department at 350 Victory Drive states that all contractors doing work in the Village of Park Forest must be Park Forest licensed. It also says a permit is required when you do the work yourself and when you hire a contractor. For anything beyond a like-for-like faucet swap, call Village Hall at 708-748-1112 and ask if your job needs one, because permits are dropped off or mailed rather than taken by email or online.

Illinois also requires people who install, alter or repair plumbing to be licensed. Plumber license numbers begin with 058, and plumbing contractors register with the state with numbers that begin with 055. You can look up a license on the Illinois Department of Public Health license verification tool before you sign anything. A contractor who cannot give you a number is a reason to keep looking, and the Village adopts the Illinois State Plumbing Code for work that does get permitted. Neighbors in Olympia Fields and Chicago Heights follow their own village or city rules, so confirm locally if the property is across the line.
