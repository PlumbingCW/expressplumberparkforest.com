Should I Get a Tankless Water Heater in Park Forest, IL?

A tankless heater suits some homes well and others poorly. Your gas line, vent path, hot water habits and how long you will stay decide which way to go.

Call (855) 575-2890
Tank vs tanklessUpfront cost driversSpace and ventingHot water habits
By the Park Forest Express Plumbing team · Published October 30, 2026

Choose tankless if you want endless hot water in a smaller footprint, can accept a higher upfront project, and your house can take the gas and vent upgrades. Stay with a tank if your budget is tight, your gas line and venting would need heavy work, or your hot water use is light and simple. Neither type is right for everyone.

The decision turns on the home itself more than the product. The table below puts the main questions side by side, and the sections after it explain each one.

Read the table by counting where you land. If most of your answers fall in one column, that column is a decent starting guess. A technician can confirm it by looking at the gas line, vent path and the heater location. That visit is also the moment to ask what the old heater's permit history looks like, since a replacement is permitted work.

What actually drives the price

We do not quote prices in articles, because the numbers depend on the house. What we can describe are the cost drivers, which explain why two homes with the same model can see very different totals. The biggest is usually everything around the unit, not the unit itself.

Gas line size

A tankless heater draws much more gas than a tank. If the existing line is too small, a bigger branch must be run.

Venting

Tankless units use sealed venting through a wall or roof. A long or awkward path costs more labor and material.

Condensate drain

Condensing models produce acidic water that must drain to a proper place, often with a neutralizer.

Electrical and water connections

Units need an outlet or circuit, plus isolation valves for flushing. Older shutoff valves are often replaced.

Permit and inspection

Time to pull a permit and schedule inspection is part of the job.

A tank replacement in the same spot often reuses the vent and gas line, which is why it tends to cost less up front. For the tank side of the choice, see our water heater replacement page.

Lifespan and running costs, in general terms

Tank heaters often last roughly 8 to 12 years, depending on water quality and care. Tankless units are commonly cited at 15 to 20 years or more with regular maintenance. Those are general figures and they vary by brand, use and water.

Tankless units skip the standby heat loss of a hot tank, which is the heat that escapes a tank all day. That helps most in homes with low or intermittent use. Savings depend on your use, and a family with heavy daily demand sees a smaller gap.

Tankless maintenance matters. Mineral scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and manufacturers recommend periodic flushing with descaling solution. Skipping it can shorten life and reduce performance. Ask whether the unit you are considering has service ports for flushing, because a unit without them is harder to maintain.

Plan around the wait, not just the capacity

Tankless heaters need a short time to start delivering, and a very low flow may not trigger them. A tank gives hot water instantly at the first draw but can run out. Think about which annoyance you would rather live with.

Space, venting and the daily experience

A wall-mounted tankless unit can free up a basement corner or closet floor. A tank takes a footprint of roughly two feet across and needs room around it for service. If the heater sits in a tight utility closet, the vent routing becomes the real question.

Daily experience differs in small ways. Tankless water arrives after a brief delay, and temperature can swing if flow changes in the middle of a shower. Tanks can run out during back-to-back showers, and recovery takes time. Hot water demand is the core of the choice, and our sizing steps live on the tankless water heater page.

If you ever smell gas near any gas appliance, leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 first. Do not hunt for the source yourself.

A short checklist before you decide

  • Note the heater's current location, vent type and gas line size.
  • List the hot water uses that overlap on your busiest morning.
  • Ask how long you plan to stay in the house.
  • Request line items for gas, vent, drain and permit work.
  • Ask how the water in your area might affect scale and flushing.

Bring photos of the heater, its vent and the gas meter to any conversation about options. If you want a technician to look at the heater location before choosing, call (855) 575-2890.

Park Forest note: ask the Water Department

Park Forest water comes from six village wells. The Village treats it at the plant by softening it with a lime and soda ash process, filtering it, adding chlorine as a disinfectant, and adding orthophosphate for corrosion control and fluoride. Softened supply can mean less scale, but the Village does not publish a hardness number on its water page, so ask rather than assume. That affects how often a tankless unit may need flushing.

Good questions for Public Works at 708-503-7702 include whether test summaries are available, what the water is like in your part of the village, and how it may affect heater life. The Water Department has extended hours to 8 pm on Mondays through Village Hall at 708-748-1112. Water heater replacement is a traditional permit item at the Building Department, so factor that in. Neighbors weighing the same choice in Richton Park have village wells 400 to 800 feet deep, and in Chicago Heights you should ask your own water supplier, since each community's supply differs.
