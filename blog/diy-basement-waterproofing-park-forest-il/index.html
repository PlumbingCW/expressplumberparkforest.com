DIY Basement Waterproofing in Park Forest, IL

Most wet basements start outside the house. Here is what you can fix yourself, what rarely works, and when to stop and call for help.

By the Park Forest Express Plumbing team · Published October 30, 2026

You can fix a surprising amount of basement water yourself. Clean gutters, downspout extensions, better grading, sealed window wells, and small crack repairs handle the most common causes at a low cost. What a homeowner cannot fix is water pushing up through the floor or through a wall under pressure, and that takes drainage work or a pump.

Start with the cheap outdoor fixes and watch what happens during the next hard rain. If the basement stays dry, you are done. If not, you have learned where the water enters, which helps when you talk to a pro about basement waterproofing.

Start by finding the real source

Tape a one-foot square of plastic sheeting to a damp wall or floor spot, sealing all four edges with duct tape. Leave it for a day or two. Moisture under the plastic means water is coming through the concrete, while moisture on the outside surface means indoor humidity is condensing.

Next, look at the pattern. Water at the base of a wall, along the floor joint, points to groundwater pressure. Streaks below a window point to the window well. A stain under a pipe or the water heater is a plumbing leak, and no sealant will fix it.

Gutters, downspouts, and grading come first

A roof sheds a lot of water in a storm, and a half-inch of rain on a 1,500 square foot roof produces hundreds of gallons. If the gutters are clogged or the downspouts empty beside the foundation, that water goes straight into the soil next to your basement wall. Clean the gutters in spring and fall, and fix any sagging or leaking sections.

Extend each downspout several feet from the house using a solid pipe or a hinged extender. Then check the ground around the foundation. It should slope away from the wall, about six inches of drop over the first ten feet, and any low spot that holds water after rain should be filled with compacted clay-rich soil, not mulch or loose topsoil.

Practical tip: watch the yard during a storm

Put on a rain jacket during the next heavy downpour and look at where the water travels. Puddles beside the foundation, downspouts that overflow, and flow running toward the house tell you more than any guess. Take photos so you can show them to a contractor.

Window wells and small cracks

Window wells fill with leaves and dirt, and then water backs up against the glass. Clear the debris, add a few inches of gravel at the bottom, and fit a clear cover that sheds rain. If the well has a drain at the bottom, pour a bucket of water in and confirm it empties.

Hairline cracks in a poured concrete wall can be sealed from inside with hydraulic cement or an injected polyurethane or epoxy kit. They are cheap and effective for narrow, stable cracks. They are not a fix for wide cracks, cracks that keep growing, or cracks with a horizontal line, which can mean the wall is moving.

Be skeptical of paint-on waterproofers. They hold back damp air, not water pressure, and they often peel off within a season or two if water keeps pushing through the wall.

Where DIY stops

Water coming up through the floor slab or the joint between wall and floor is groundwater pressure, and surface fixes do not change it. The common professional answer is an interior drain, a perimeter drain tile with a sump pit, and a pump to push the water out. That job requires cutting the floor, and it is not a weekend project for most homeowners.

A wall that bows inward, a horizontal crack across a block wall, or stair-step cracks in the mortar are structural problems. A plumber or waterproofing contractor can describe the water side of the fix, but a structural engineer should look at the wall itself. Do not seal a moving wall and hope it holds.

If a drain or sewer line is backing up into a floor drain, that is a different problem and not groundwater. A wet corner in a home in Olympia Fields or Chicago Heights can turn out to be a drain problem too, so find out which before you spend money on sealers.

Keep the basement dry between storms

  • Run a dehumidifier in summer to hold humidity below about 50 percent.
  • Keep storage a few inches off the floor on plastic shelves.
  • Look at the sump pit twice a year and pour in water to test the float.
  • Leave the discharge line clear of leaves, ice, and snow piles.
  • Check the water heater and washer hoses for slow drips that look like seepage.

These habits cost little and catch small problems before they become damaged drywall or stained carpet. Mold can start growing on damp materials within a day or two, so remove wet items quickly after a storm.

Park Forest programs that help keep water out

The Village of Park Forest says urban flooding happens when rain falls faster than the soil can take it in, and the sewers cannot handle the runoff. That is why the Village promotes simple yard fixes. Its programs include downspout extensions, rain barrels, a rain garden incentive, and permeable pavement, all of which keep roof and driveway runoff from reaching your foundation.

The park system was designed as reservoirs for stormwater, and Central Park was restored as a wetland, so the Village treats yards and parks as part of the drainage plan. The Village also offers a Flood Factor map where you can check an address. It is a modeled estimate, not a Village measurement, but it can help you decide how seriously to take a soft spot in your yard. For sewage backups, remember that the Village is replacing its own wastewater lines, so a backup at your home may still be on your private side and worth a camera look.
